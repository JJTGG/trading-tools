Foundations

Start here

What is trading?

Trading is the buying and selling of financial instruments with the aim of managing exposure, transferring risk, or attempting to benefit from price movement.

Market participants

Retail traders, institutions, market makers, brokers, exchanges, funds, and other participants interact to create orders, liquidity, and price discovery.

Price discovery

Prices emerge from the interaction of buy and sell orders. Bid, ask, spread, depth, and available liquidity all affect how that process behaves.

Trading plans

A trading plan defines what is being traded, why the position exists, where invalidation occurs, how much is at risk, and what conditions change the plan.

02

Markets

Where the numbers come from

Spot trading

Spot markets involve buying or selling the underlying instrument rather than a derivative contract built from it.

Margin and derivatives

Margin allows a trader to control exposure with less capital than the full position value. Futures and perpetual contracts add leverage and liquidation risk.

Forex markets

Forex instruments quote one currency against another. Price conventions, spread, position size, and pip calculations depend on the pair and trading environment.

Crypto markets

Cryptocurrency markets operate across centralized and decentralized venues, often with significant differences in liquidity, volatility, and market structure.

Equity markets

Stocks represent ownership interests in companies. Trading conditions can vary by exchange, session, instrument, liquidity, and corporate events.

03

Trading methods

Different ways to participate

Day trading

Positions are generally opened and closed within the same trading session, making execution, volatility, and short-term risk especially important.

Swing trading

Swing traders typically hold positions across multiple sessions and focus on larger price movements than purely intraday strategies.

Position trading

Position trading generally uses longer holding periods, with decisions based on broader market structure, trends, fundamentals, or other long-horizon factors.

Arbitrage

Arbitrage strategies attempt to capture price differences between related markets or venues, while accounting for fees, latency, liquidity, and execution.

Algorithmic trading

Algorithmic systems use explicit rules or models to analyze markets, generate signals, manage orders, or automate parts of a trading process.

Options

Options give the holder defined rights under a contract. Strike price, premium, expiration, volatility, and time all affect their behavior and risk.

04

Risk management

Define the downside first

Position sizing

Position size connects account risk to the distance between entry and invalidation. The same percentage risk can require very different position sizes.

Open Position Size

Risk / reward

Risk / reward compares the distance to a defined loss point with the distance to a defined target. It describes the setup; it does not predict the outcome.

Open Risk / Reward

Leverage

Leverage changes the amount of market exposure relative to available capital. Increasing exposure also changes the consequences of adverse price movement.

Open Leverage

PnL

Profit and loss can be measured from entry, exit, direction, position size, and fees. Net results matter more than a price move viewed in isolation.

Open PnL

Volatility

Volatility describes how much and how quickly prices move. Higher volatility can increase both opportunity and the size of normal adverse movement.

Liquidity and slippage

Thin liquidity can increase spreads, slippage, and execution uncertainty. Position size should be considered alongside actual market depth.

05

Psychology & behavior

The human part of the system

Fear and greed

Emotional reactions can affect entries, exits, sizing, and willingness to follow previously defined rules.

FOMO and chasing

Entering because a move already happened can change the original risk structure of a trade. The resulting position may no longer resemble the planned setup.

Confirmation bias

Traders can unconsciously prefer information that supports an existing view. Deliberately defining invalidation helps make the analysis less one-sided.

Revenge trading

Increasing risk because of a previous loss changes the decision process. Recording trades makes it easier to examine whether sizing or behavior is drifting.

Review and journaling

Recording what was known, what was planned, what happened, and what changed creates a basis for reviewing decisions rather than only outcomes.

06

Technical concepts

The market vocabulary

Price and volume

Price shows where transactions occur while volume provides information about trading activity. Interpretation depends on the instrument and market structure.

Support and resistance

Traders often use recurring areas of buying or selling interest as reference levels. These are analytical constructs, not guarantees that price will reverse.

Trend and structure

Market structure can be described using sequences of highs and lows, ranges, breakouts, and changes in directional behavior.

Timeframes

The same instrument can display very different behavior across timeframes. A setup's timeframe should match the way the position is being managed.
